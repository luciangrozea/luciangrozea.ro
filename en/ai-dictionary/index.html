Part I

Foundations

What artificial intelligence actually is, and how a machine manages to learn.

01

Artificial intelligence

AI

DefinitionThe field that builds computer programs able to do things that normally require human intelligence: understanding language, recognizing images, making decisions or solving problems.

In plain words

Think of AI as an extremely fast apprentice. It doesn't “think” like a person and it isn't conscious, but it has been trained to imitate certain human abilities: seeing, listening, reading, answering. Sometimes it is astonishingly good; other times it makes mistakes a child wouldn't.

Example

When your phone recognizes your face to unlock, when a streaming platform recommends what to watch tonight, or when you chat with an assistant like ChatGPT or Claude, there is an AI system working behind the scenes.

See also: Machine learning, Generative AI

02

Algorithm

DefinitionA list of clear steps, in a specific order, that a computer follows to solve a problem.

In plain words

An algorithm is like a cake recipe: “Beat 3 eggs. Add 200 g of sugar. Mix for 2 minutes. Bake for 30 minutes at 180°C.” Follow the steps exactly and you get the same result every time. Computers are excellent at following recipes: they never get bored and never skip a step.

Example

Your navigation app uses an algorithm to find the fastest route: it compares the possible routes step by step and picks the best one. The difference from AI: here every rule was written by a programmer. In AI, many of the rules are discovered by the computer itself.

See also: Machine learning

03

Machine learning

ML

DefinitionA branch of AI in which the computer isn't given rules written by people; instead, it learns rules and patterns on its own by studying lots of examples.

In plain words

How do you explain to a child what a cat is? You don't give them a definition (“a mammal with whiskers and pointy ears…”). You show them lots of cats and, at some point, they recognize one they've never seen before. Machine learning works the same way: you give the computer thousands of examples and it figures out on its own what they have in common.

Example

Your email's spam filter has “seen” millions of messages labelled as spam or not spam. From them it learned by itself what a suspicious message looks like, without anyone writing every single rule.

See also: Training data, Model, Neural network

04

Training data

DefinitionThe collection of examples (texts, images, sounds, numbers) that an AI system learns from.

In plain words

Training data is the AI's set of textbooks and workbooks. A student who learned from good, varied textbooks will know more than one who only had a few torn pages. Likewise, an AI is only as good as the data it learned from. If the textbooks contain mistakes, it will learn those too.

Example

To recognize skin conditions in photos, a medical AI is trained on tens of thousands of images checked by dermatologists, each labelled with the correct diagnosis.

See also: Model, Bias

05

Model

AI model

DefinitionThe result of the learning process: a program that has “absorbed” the patterns in the training data and can apply them to new situations.

In plain words

If training data is the textbooks, the model is the student who has graduated. They no longer carry the textbooks, but they kept what they learned and can answer new questions. When you hear about GPT, Claude or Gemini, think of different “graduates” trained at different schools.

Example

An app that tells you which plant you photographed uses a model: you give it a new photo and it replies “This is lavender”, based on what it learned from millions of other photos.

See also: Parameters, Inference, LLM

06

Neural network

DefinitionA type of model loosely inspired by the human brain, made of many artificial “neurons” connected to each other and organized in layers that pass information along.

In plain words

Picture a team of workers in a factory, seated in rows. Each one receives a few notes from colleagues in the row behind, makes a tiny decision (“this looks important” or “this doesn't”) and passes it on. None of them understands the whole problem, but together, row after row, they reach the right answer.

Example

When you show it a photo, the first layer of “neurons” only sees patches of light and shadow, the middle layers combine those patches into shapes, and the last layer decides: “It's a dog.”

See also: Deep learning, Parameters

07

Deep learning

DefinitionMachine learning that uses neural networks with many layers (“deep” networks). It is the technology behind almost every spectacular breakthrough in AI in recent years.

In plain words

Think of a building with many floors. On the ground floor you only see lines. On the second floor, lines become shapes: circles, corners. On the third, shapes become eyes, a nose, a mouth. On the top floor it all comes together: “This is a face.” The more floors the building has, the more complex the things it can understand.

Example

High-quality machine translation, voice recognition on your phone, self-driving cars and modern chatbots all use deep learning.

See also: Neural network, Transformer and attention

08

Parameters

DefinitionThe adjustable numbers inside a model that change during training. Everything the model has learned is, in fact, “stored” in them.

In plain words

Imagine a mixing desk like the ones at concerts, but with billions of knobs instead of a few dozen. During training, every knob is turned a little at a time until the sound (the model's answers) comes out as well as possible. The final position of all the knobs is what the model “knows”.

Example

When you read that a model has “70 billion parameters”, it means it has 70 billion such knobs. In general, more parameters means a model capable of more complex things, but also more expensive to run.

See also: Model, Fine-tuning

Part II

Language models

How ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and the other assistants work under the hood.

09

Generative AI

DefinitionThe kind of AI that creates new content (text, images, music, video or code) instead of only analyzing or classifying existing content.

In plain words

A “classic” AI is like an art critic: it looks at a painting and tells you its style. A generative AI is like a painter: it has studied thousands of paintings and can paint a new one that never existed, in the style you ask for.

Example

You type “an orange cat reading the newspaper in a Paris café, watercolor style” and get a brand-new image in a few seconds. Or you ask a chatbot to write a congratulations message for a colleague.

See also: LLM, Multimodal

10

LLM

Large Language Model

DefinitionA huge AI model trained on enormous amounts of text, which has learned to understand and generate natural language. It is the “engine” behind chatbots like ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini.

In plain words

Imagine someone who has read almost everything ever written on the internet: books, articles, forums, recipes, code. They don't remember every page, but they've become extraordinarily good at one thing: cleverly guessing the next suitable word. Repeated hundreds of times, this “game” produces answers, essays and entire conversations.

Example

If you type “The capital of France is…”, an LLM knows the most likely next word is “Paris”. The very same mechanism, at a huge scale, lets it explain photosynthesis or polish your CV.

See also: Token, Transformer and attention, Prompt

11

Transformer and attention

DefinitionThe architecture (the way a model is built) used by almost all modern LLMs. Its key idea is called “attention”: the model looks at all the words in a text at once and decides which ones matter for understanding each of the others.

In plain words

When you read “Mike gave Anna the book because she asked for it”, you instantly know that “she” means Anna, not Mike. Your mind has “drawn a line” between those two words. The attention mechanism does exactly that: it draws thousands of invisible lines, like a highlighter connecting the words that matter to each other.

Example

The “T” in “ChatGPT” stands for Transformer. The architecture was introduced by Google researchers in 2017, in a paper with a now-famous title: “Attention Is All You Need”.

See also: LLM, Token

12

Token

DefinitionThe small chunk of text an LLM works with. A token can be a whole word, part of a word or a punctuation mark.

In plain words

The model doesn't read letters or whole words, but “Lego bricks” of text. Short, common words are a single brick, while long or rare ones are built from several. Everything the AI reads and writes is really just a chain of such bricks.

Example

The sentence “Hello, my friend!” might be split roughly like this: [Hello] [,] [ my] [ friend] [!]. It matters because AI services are billed by the number of tokens, and the “memory” of a conversation (see Context window) is measured in tokens too. In English, 100 tokens are roughly 75 words; languages such as Romanian usually need a few more.

See also: Context window, LLM

13

Context window

DefinitionThe maximum amount of text (measured in tokens) an AI model can “see” and take into account at once: the whole conversation so far, any attached documents, and the answer it is currently writing.

In plain words

Imagine the AI works at a desk. On the desk it keeps every document from your conversation: what you wrote, what it replied, the files you gave it. As long as everything fits on the desk, it can consult anything. But the desk has a fixed size. When it gets too full, the oldest documents start falling off the edge, which is why, in very long conversations, the AI sometimes seems to “forget” what you discussed at the beginning.

Example

Modern models have very large desks: some can hold hundreds of thousands of tokens, the equivalent of several novels. Still, if you work for hours in the same conversation, it's a good idea to start a fresh one now and then, beginning with a short summary of what matters.

See also: Token, RAG

14

Inference

DefinitionThe moment an already-trained model is actually used: it receives a new question and produces an answer.

In plain words

Training is like a football team's months of pre-season camp: it takes long, costs a lot and happens rarely. Inference is the match itself, where the team uses what it practised. Every question you ask a chatbot is a small “match”.

Example

Training a top LLM can take months and cost tens or even hundreds of millions of dollars. Inference, the answer to your question, takes a few seconds and usually costs a fraction of a cent.

See also: Model, Temperature

Part III

Talking to AI

What you tell it, how you tell it, and what to watch out for in the answer.

15

Prompt

DefinitionThe instruction, question or text you give an AI model in order to get a response.

In plain words

A prompt is like an order at a restaurant. The chef (the AI) can cook almost anything, but will cook what's written on the ticket. “Something to eat” will bring you a surprise; “a vegetable soup, no onions, not too hot” brings you exactly what you wanted.

Example

“Write me a short poem about the sea” is a prompt. So are “Summarize this document in 5 points” or a photo accompanied by the question “What plant is this?”.

See also: Prompt engineering, Context window

16

Prompt engineering

DefinitionThe art of writing clear, well-structured prompts so the AI gives the most useful and precise answers possible.

In plain words

Think of the difference between telling a new colleague “Make me a presentation” and “Make me a 10-slide presentation for clients about our Q3 results, in an upbeat tone, with a chart on slide 4”. The second one has every chance of bringing you exactly what you wanted. It's the same with AI: context, goal, format and examples make all the difference.

Example

Instead of “Write me a CV”, a good prompt would be: “You are an experienced IT recruiter. Based on the information below, write a one-page CV for a Junior AI Engineer role. Emphasize the personal projects and use a professional tone.”

See also: Prompt, Hallucination

17

Temperature

DefinitionA setting that controls how predictable or, on the contrary, how creative and surprising a model's answers are.

In plain words

Imagine a thermostat for imagination. At a low temperature, the AI always picks the safest, most likely word, like a meticulous accountant. At a high temperature, it allows itself unexpected words, like a poet improvising. Turn the thermostat up too far and it starts saying things that make no sense.

Example

For calculations, extracting data from an invoice or technical answers, a low temperature is used. For brainstorming, slogans or children's stories, a higher temperature brings more varied ideas.

See also: Hallucination, Inference

18

Hallucination

DefinitionWhen an AI model generates false or made-up information but presents it with full confidence, as if it were true.

In plain words

It's like a student in an oral exam who doesn't know the answer but doesn't want to stay silent. So they improvise, fluently and confidently, something that sounds plausible. The difference is that the AI isn't lying on purpose: it produces the text that sounds most likely, and sometimes the likely text simply isn't true.

Example

You ask for a list of books on a topic, and the AI includes a title that doesn't exist, with an invented author and publication year. That's why important information (medical, legal, financial, figures, quotes) should always be checked against reliable sources.

See also: RAG, Temperature

19

Multimodal

Multimodal AI

DefinitionAn AI model's ability to understand and work with several kinds of information at once: text, images, sound, video.

In plain words

The first chatbots could only “read”, like someone who communicates exclusively through written notes. A multimodal model has both “eyes” and “ears”: it can look at a photo, listen to a recording and reply in writing or by voice, all in the same conversation.

Example

You take a photo of what's in your fridge and ask “What can I cook with this?”. The model recognizes the ingredients in the image and suggests three recipes.

See also: Generative AI, LLM

Part IV

AI in practice

The techniques that make AI genuinely useful at work and in everyday life.

20

Fine-tuning

DefinitionThe process of training an already-trained model a little more, on a smaller set of specialized data, so it gets better at a particular task or field.

In plain words

A medical graduate already knows a lot about the human body in general. To become a cardiologist, they don't start from scratch: they specialize in the heart. Fine-tuning is the AI's specialization: it starts from a general model and gives it extra training for a specific field.

Example

A bank can take a general model and fine-tune it on thousands of real customer conversations, so that it answers in the bank's style and knows its products well.

See also: Model, RAG

21

Embeddings

Vector representations

DefinitionA way of turning words, sentences or images into lists of numbers, so that things with similar meanings get similar numbers.

In plain words

Imagine a huge map of meanings. Every word gets coordinates on this map. “Dog” and “cat” live on the same street, “car” and “truck” are in another neighborhood, and “joy” and “happiness” are next-door neighbors. The computer doesn't understand the words, but it can measure the distance between them on the map.

Example

Thanks to embeddings, when you search an online store for “running footwear”, you also get results for “jogging sneakers”, even though they don't contain the exact words you typed: their meaning is close.

See also: RAG

22

RAG

Retrieval-Augmented Generation

DefinitionA technique where, before answering, the AI searches a collection of documents (a knowledge base) for relevant information and builds its answer on it.

In plain words

It's the difference between a closed-book and an open-book exam. Without RAG, the AI answers from memory alone, and the information may be wrong or outdated. With RAG, it first looks up the right page in the “book”, reads it, then answers. The result: more accurate answers, with sources you can check.

Example

A telecom company's support chatbot uses RAG: when you ask “How much does roaming in Turkey cost?”, it searches the official documents for the current rate and answers, pointing to the exact document it took the information from.

See also: Embeddings, Hallucination, AI agent

23

AI agent

DefinitionAn AI system that doesn't just answer questions but acts on its own to get a task done: it makes a plan, uses tools (browser, email, calendar, files), checks the result and corrects itself until it's finished.

In plain words

A regular chatbot is like a consultant giving advice: “You should book a flight on Tuesday.” An agent is like a personal assistant who actually books the flight: it searches websites, compares prices, checks your calendar and sends you the confirmation. You give it the goal; it works out the steps.

Example

You tell an agent: “Find me three Italian restaurants nearby, open on Saturday evening, with good reviews, and put them in a table.” The agent searches the web, reads the reviews, checks opening hours and delivers the table. Even this website was built with the help of an AI agent that wrote the code, tested it and published it.

See also: Prompt, Alignment

Part V

Responsible AI

Why it matters that AI is fair, honest and safe.

24

Bias

DefinitionAn AI system's tendency to produce unfair or unbalanced results, usually because the data it learned from was itself unbalanced.

In plain words

If a child grew up seeing only pictures where doctors are men and nurses are women, they might come to believe “that's just how it is”. AI learns from our data, so it also picks up the prejudices in it, like a scale that was tilted from the very start.

Example

An AI for screening CVs, trained on the hiring history of a company that has mostly hired men, may end up scoring women's CVs lower. To prevent this, the data and the results need to be checked carefully and rebalanced.

See also: Training data, Alignment

25

Alignment

AI alignment

DefinitionThe effort to make AI systems pursue human goals and values: to be helpful, honest and not cause harm.

In plain words

A very capable AI is like a very powerful car: the power is only useful if the steering and brakes work and the car goes where you want it to. Alignment means building that steering wheel: the model must understand not only what you said, but what you actually meant, and refuse dangerous things.

Example

If you ask an AI “Help me lose 10 kg in a week”, a well-aligned model won't hand you a dangerous diet just to fulfil the request. It will explain why the goal isn't healthy and suggest a safe alternative.

See also: Bias, AI agent
